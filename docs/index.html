From an afternoon build to a university airframe.

01

Seedling

DIY · about $30–150

A water rocket and an A–D model rocket with printed ring tails and a printed folding rotor. The motor's own ejection charge frees the rotor, so there is nothing to wire.

  • Build in an afternoon
  • Target: 10 flights per airframe
  • CAD and ground tests ready
02

Sapling

University team · high power

A fiberglass or carbon airframe with a carbon ring tail. Commercial dual-deploy altimeters release a drogue, then the rotor, and every flight keeps a backup parachute.

  • Level 1 to Level 3 certified motors
  • Target: 5 flights per airframe
  • Paper and simulation today
03

Canopy

Full scale · paper study

An honest look at what a ring-tail, rotor-recovered orbital launcher would gain and lose, set against propulsive landing. Rotary Rocket's Roton tried rotors in 1999; Falcon 9 and New Shepard won with engines.

  • Public-textbook depth only
  • Held for export-control review
  • Not part of this release

The lineup

Six configurations, one framework.

Every stage lands on its own. Built from shared modules in sim/lineup.py. Each is checked against the scope rules in code: certified motors only, no guidance, a backup parachute on every rotor stage. Apogees are vertical-flight upper bounds; real flights land lower. Bars use a logarithmic scale.

Why it spins

It spins because it falls.

Air rushing up through the rotor turns it, and the turning blades hold the rocket up. That is autorotation, the same thing a maple seed does. The blades are fixed at 6° of pitch, which the blade-element model found to be the best setting. In descent the rotor acts like a disc with a drag coefficient of about 1.08. A round parachute is about 1.5, so a rotor needs to be a little wider to land as softly. Drone drop tests will measure the real number.

Ring tail, honestly

In the sim a ring tail costs about 16–17% of apogee against fins of the same stability. In return it is compact, stiff and hard to break on landing. Stability is proven with simulation and swing tests and shown to the range safety officer (RSO).

Rotor, honestly

Descent is about the same as a parachute's, with less fabric but more mechanism and spin loads. We measure it against a parachute instead of assuming it is better, and every high-power Maplewing keeps a backup parachute.

History, honestly

Rotary Rocket's Roton flew rotor tests in 1999 and was abandoned. Propulsive landing won at full scale. Maplewing is about small reusable rockets that are fun to fly again, not a claim to beat Falcon 9.

Print the parts

Parametric CAD, ready to print.

Pick a part to turn it in 3D or download its STL. Drawn in CadQuery from cad/maplewing_parts.py. Measure your body tube or bottle first and change the parameters, because the fit is set for 24.8 mm BT-50 tube and a 105 mm bottle. Print in PETG with thin walls, and cut blades from balsa sheet to the outline. Masses are from the CAD; red ones are over the simulator's budget and the sim already uses the heavier figure.

The equations

The physics, in the open.

The simulator runs these equations in Python and in your browser, and a test in CI checks that both give the same answers.

Flight

Vertical, multi-stage, explicit Euler at 5 ms. Thrust is the certified curve from ThrustCurve.org; motor mass falls with impulse delivered.

Stability

Extended Barrowman. The ring tail is treated as panels of span , a community method that swing tests will check.

Rotor

Blade-element autorotation: find the spin rate where torque is zero, then the descent speed where thrust equals weight. Best fixed pitch 6°.

Water

Adiabatic water phase, then choked and unchoked air blow-down. 60 psi in flight, proof-tested full of water at 90 psi.

Scope and safety

Hard rules.

Pull requests that break them are closed.

  • Commercial certified motors and passive, unguided rockets only, flown under the National Association of Rocketry (NAR) or Tripoli Rocketry Association (TRA) safety codes at sanctioned launches.
  • No propellant or motor making, no energetics handling, no DIY pyro firing circuits, no active guidance, no steerable fins and no actively controlled rotor pitch.
  • Recovery deploys by motor ejection (Seedling) or redundant commercial altimeters (Sapling). Rotors are fixed-pitch and passive. The logger only records.
  • Water rockets: rated plastic bottles only, a tested pressure limit with margin and a remote release. Never metal or glass.
  • Full-scale work stays at public-textbook depth and goes through export-control review before anything is published.
